# Project-level decision framework

The national footprint is context. A local decision needs project evidence.

## 1. Define the proposed commitment
Record initial and ultimate electric demand, annual and peak water demand, cooling design, backup source, expected operating profile, construction phases, and the date each phase may come online.

## 2. Identify the real source path
Trace water from the facility back through the utility, well, reservoir, reclaimed-water system, purchased supply, or interbasin transfer. Trace electricity to the relevant grid region and infrastructure constraints. A map boundary under the building is not enough.

## 3. Test difficult conditions
Use a range of credible conditions rather than one annual average: hot weather, drought restrictions, source outage, treatment limitation, coincident community peak demand, and committed future loads.

## 4. Separate facts from scenarios
Measured operating data, executed contracts, utility studies, applicant forecasts, and illustrative calculations should remain labeled as different evidence classes.

## 5. Stage expansion when uncertainty is material
A project does not need a perfect twenty-year forecast. It can be structured so later capacity is conditional on agreed performance and capacity milestones. The exact milestones must be defined before the result is known.

## 6. Scale financial protection to exposure
Security should track the project-driven cost that other customers could be left carrying. Avoid both extremes: socializing a large project-specific risk and demanding redundant or disproportionate collateral that blocks ordinary entrants.

## 7. Identify enforcement authority
For every critical commitment, record which entity can enforce it, what instrument creates the obligation, what survives a sale or restructuring, and what remedy exists if the condition fails.

## 8. Measure the service delivered
For claims that computing creates a resource saving, define the counterfactual and count the full workflow: attempts, checks, retries, fallbacks, professional review where needed, and downstream repair. New capabilities can be valuable even when there is no avoided manual task; they simply should not be mislabeled as avoided resource use.
